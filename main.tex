\section{Background and Significance}
%% why stigma is important
Stigma occurs when labeling and stereotyping co-occur with separation, status loss, and discrimination in a context of unequal power.\cite{link2001} It contributes to population health inequalities by shaping access to material, social, and healthcare resources through structural, interpersonal, and psychological pathways.\cite{hatzenbuehler2013} In healthcare, these processes can operate through clinical notes, as clinicians hold the authority to label patients in a record that patients can rarely contest.%Clinical notes is one way these effects may operate. Stigmatizing descriptions entered into the record have been shown to worsen provider attitudes and pain-management decisions.
\cite{goddu2018} Once recorded, the stigmatizing language stays in the patient's longitudinal record, where it can shape how later clinicians interpret symptoms, judge credibility, and allocate treatment.\cite{goddu2018,park2021} Patients who read their own notes may also encounter it directly, which can undermine their trust and engagement with the healthcare system.\cite{leveille2020notes} The clinical notes can therefore pass stigma from one clinician to the next and to patients themselves.
 
%% stigma can be different ... previous ...
Stigmatizing language in clinical records takes multiple forms, including expressions of doubt and disapproval, negative stereotyping, difficult-patient framing, and assertions of clinician authority.\cite{park2021,mcarthur2026} Its prevalence also varies across patient populations, studies have found higher rates of stigmatizing language in notes for patients with substance use disorders, chronic pain, and disability compared with other conditions, as well as disparities by recorded race and ethnicity.\cite{himmelstein2022, sun2022} \td{However, these studies treat the note as a single unit, although clinical documentation is written for more than one reader.} %Clinical documentation is also not written for a single reader. 
Discharge summaries, for example, contain sections written mainly for clinicians alongside sections written directly for patients.\cite{li2026readability} \td{Whole-note estimates therefore cannot show whether stigmatizing language persists in text that patients read, or whether the disparities across populations differ by audience.} %Existing studies have largely examined clinician-facing narratives without distinguishing whether their findings extend to patient-facing text.

%% introducing section diff
%This distinction matters because clinical documentation serves different audiences and functions.\cite{li2026readability} 
Within a discharge summary, the Brief Hospital Course (BHC) and History of Present Illness (HPI) are clinician-facing, which describe the patient's clinical history and hospital encounter primarily for other clinicians, whereas the Discharge Instructions (DI) is patient-facing that communicates information about follow-up care, medications, precautions, and self-care to patients.\cite{xu2024dischargeme,li2026readability} Stigmatizing language in clinician-facing sections may frame subsequent clinical interpretation and influence treatment decisions.\cite{goddu2018} In DI, such language becomes part of direct patient-facing communication, where patients may encounter wording they perceive as judgmental, \cite{leveille2020notes,li2026readability} which can undermine trust at the very point where patients are asked to follow care instructions. To our knowledge, prior work has not compared patient-facing DI with clinician-facing sections from the same discharge summary. %It therefore remains unclear whether stigmatizing language becomes less common, or takes a different form, when clinicians write directly for patients. 
Addressing this would clarify how clinicians adapt their language to different audiences and how stigmatizing language propagates into patient-facing communication, informing both clinical documentation training and the design of models that detect stigmatizing language or generate discharge instructions.

%% intro nlp model used in stigma detection
Natural language processing has made it possible to identify stigmatizing language at scale.\cite{harrigian2023,xue2026,walker2025,scroggins2025} Initial approaches combined domain-informed lexicons with contextual classifiers,\cite{harrigian2023} and later work extended detection through transfer learning, expanded classification frameworks, and domain-specific models.\cite{xue2026,walker2025,scroggins2025} Recently, large language models have also been applied to detecting stigmatizing language and to auditing clinical documentation more generally.\cite{sethi2026,dahal2026} 
These studies have typically analyzed stigmatizing language at the level of whole notes or isolated sentences, without distinguishing the sections within a note or the audiences they are written for, and thus %These studies have generally treated the clinical note, or individual sentences within it, as a single undifferentiated unit and have not modeled the intended audience of each section. Prevalence estimates pooled across a document 
cannot show whether stigmatizing language differs between clinician-facing and patient-facing text.

Our goal was to identify where and for whom patients are most likely to encounter stigmatizing language in discharge documentation. Analyzing 259,207 discharge summaries from MIMIC-IV-Note,\cite{mimicnote2023,mimiciv2024} we first compared the prevalence of stigmatizing language between clinician-facing (BHC and HPI) and patient-facing (DI) sections. We then examined how prevalence in patient-facing DI varied across demographic groups and disease cohorts, indicating which patients are most exposed. Finally, we examined how the clinician-to-patient gap varied across the same groups, indicating where stigmatizing language is least filtered when clinicians write for patients.

The corpus was built from a lexicon of 89 stigma-related keywords in 3 categories: Credibility \& Obstinacy, Compliance, and Descriptors.\td{maybe briefly clarify what each means?} Each instance consists of a sentence containing one of these keywords, together with the sentence before and after it as context. We examined eight disease cohorts: substance use disorder, serious mental illness, sickle cell disease, chronic pain, obesity, HIV, end-stage renal disease, and diabetes. The BHC, HPI, and DI sections were withheld so that cohort verification was independent of the sections in which stigmatizing language was measured. Each model returned one of three labels: SUPPORTED, if the provided sections contained evidence that the patient had the condition; REFUTED, if they contained evidence that the patient did not; or INSUFFICIENT, if they did not contain enough information to decide. \subsubsection{Comparison between sections}
\td{model 1 does not make much sense, just use the subtitle to show what this section aims to do. }
We aimed to compare the frequency of stigmatizing language across BHC, HPI, and DI, and to quantify the differences between sections. Because sections varied in length, comparing the raw number of stigmatizing sentences would not provide a fair comparison. Instead, we compared the number of stigmatizing sentences relative to the total word count, expressed as the number of stigmatizing sentences per 1,000 words. We used rate ratios (RRs) to quantify these differences. For example, an RR of 8 for BHC versus DI indicates that stigmatizing sentences occurred eight times as frequently per word in BHC as in DI.

We estimated RRs and their 95\% confidence intervals (CIs) using Poisson regression,\cite{mccullagh1989} a statistical method for modeling event counts. The analysis dataset contained one row per section, resulting in three rows (BHC, HPI, and DI) for each discharge summary. Each row recorded the number of stigmatizing sentences, the total word count, the section type, and the note identifier. The model used the number of stigmatizing sentences as the outcome and section type as the predictor. Word count was incorporated as an offset to account for differences in section length.

We treated DI as the reference category and represented section type using two binary indicator variables: \(BHC_i\) was coded as 1 for BHC sections and 0 otherwise, while \(HPI_i\) was coded as 1 for HPI sections and 0 otherwise. Thus, both indicators were 0 for DI sections. The model was specified as:
\begin{equation}
\log(\mathbb{E}[Y_i]) =
\beta_0 + \beta_1 BHC_i + \beta_2 HPI_i
+ \log(W_i),
\end{equation}
where \(Y_i\) is the number of stigmatizing sentences and \(W_i\) is the total word count in section \(i\). Here, \(\beta_0\) represents the log rate for DI, while \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\) represent the differences in log rates between BHC and DI, and HPI and DI, respectively. Exponentiating these coefficients gives the corresponding RRs:
\begin{equation}
RR_{BHC} = \exp(\beta_1), \qquad
RR_{HPI} = \exp(\beta_2).
\end{equation}
Thus, an RR greater than 1 indicates a higher frequency of stigmatizing sentences in the clinician-facing section than in DI, whereas an RR below 1 indicates a lower frequency.

Because the three sections within each discharge summary came from the same patient and admission and were written by the same clinician, their observations could be correlated rather than independent. To account for this, we fitted the Poisson model using generalized estimating equations (GEE),\cite{zeger1986} with observations clustered by note identifier. This approach accounts for the correlation between sections from the same summary when estimating standard errors and 95\% CIs, while retaining the same interpretation of the RRs. %No additional patient-, admission-, or author-level covariates were included because these characteristics were shared across the three sections of each summary.\cite{zeger1986} 
Further details of the statistical model are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

\subsubsection{Differences across patient groups}
We next asked whether the gap between BHC and DI differs across patient groups. For each group, we computed the BHC versus DI RR among patients in the group and among all other patients, and compared the two as a ratio, the interaction RR. For example, if the BHC versus DI RR is 10 among patients with substance use disorder and 5 among other patients, the interaction RR is 10/5 = 2. The disease results show where patients read the most stigmatizing language. Substance use disorder, serious mental illness, and sickle cell disease had DI rates 3.1 to 4.3 times the all-note rate, and their gaps between BHC and DI were significantly narrower. Their BHC rates were also above the all-note rate, so the narrower gaps came from more stigmatizing language in the instructions these patients received. Earlier work found stigmatizing language concentrated in notes for patients with substance use disorders and showed that it changed clinicians' attitudes and pain management for a patient with sickle cell disease.\cite{himmelstein2022,goddu2018} For these patients, the language also appears in the text written for them.

One explanation is that discharge instructions for these patients more often address substance use, pain control, medication adherence, or crisis plans, topics in which these keywords are common. Obesity showed the opposite pattern, its gap was wider because its DI rate was the lowest of the eight cohorts while its BHC rate was close to the all-note rate, which would follow if instructions for these patients seldom discuss the behaviors the lexicon describes. Examining which keywords occur in DI within each cohort would test these explanations.

These results also show why the interaction RR should be reported with the absolute rates. Here a narrower gap meant that patient-facing text carried more stigmatizing language, and the interaction RRs alone would have hidden the cohorts whose instructions contained the most.

\subsection{Implications for informatics practice}

Our findings have three practical implications for clinical documentation audits and natural language processing tools.\cite{himmelstein2022,walker2025,scroggins2025} First, because the rate of stigmatizing language in BHC was 8.21 times the rate in DI, documentation-equity audits should report rates by section rather than pooling across the whole note, which confounds section-specific language use with section length. Second, because clinicians used lexicon keywords at different frequencies across sections and the same keyword was more often neutral in DI than in BHC, automated detection and authoring-support tools should evaluate full sentences in context and calibrate thresholds by section rather than flagging isolated keywords. Third, because stigmatizing language in DI appeared in only 1.16\% of notes overall but was three to four times as common in substance use disorder, serious mental illness, and sickle cell disease, quality-improvement reviews of patient-facing instructions should target these high-prevalence cohorts rather than deploying blanket alerts across all discharge summaries.
