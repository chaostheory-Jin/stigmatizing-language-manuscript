\abstract{\textbf{Objective:} Stigmatizing language in clinical notes can influence clinicians' judgments and treatment decisions, with potential consequences for patient outcomes. Its use may also depend on whom a section of the note is written for. We aimed to quantify differences in stigmatizing language between clinician-facing and patient-facing sections and to examine how these differences varied across patient cohorts.\\
\textbf{Materials and Methods:} We analyzed 259,207 discharge summaries from MIMIC-IV-Note, extracting the clinician-facing Brief Hospital Course (BHC) and History of Present Illness (HPI) sections and the patient-facing Discharge Instructions (DI) section. A fine-tuned language-model classifier labeled keyword-containing sentences as stigmatizing or non-stigmatizing. Disease cohorts defined by diagnosis codes were verified by five large language models (LLMs), and only conditions verified by all five were analyzed. Poisson generalized estimating equation models compared rates of stigmatizing language across sections and examined whether the differences varied across patient cohorts.\\
\textbf{Results:} The stigma classifier achieved a test macro-F1 score of 0.928. The rate of stigmatizing language in BHC was 8.21 times the rate in DI (95\% CI, 7.96-8.47; $P<.001$), and the rate in HPI was 7.21 times the rate in DI (95\% CI, 6.94-7.50; $P<.001$). Substance use disorder, serious mental illness, and sickle cell disease had the highest rates in patient-facing instructions and significantly narrower differences between clinician-facing and patient-facing sections, whereas obesity and diabetes widened the difference.\\
\textbf{Discussion:} Stigmatizing language differed sharply by the intended audience of a section. Whole-note estimates combine clinician-facing narrative with patient-facing instructions, which hides where the language appears and how patterns across patient groups differ between the two.\\
\textbf{Conclusion:} Measuring stigmatizing language by section separates what clinicians write for one another from what patients are given to read, and shows where audience-specific documentation review could focus.}

\section{Background and Significance}
%% why stigma is important
Stigma occurs when labeling and stereotyping co-occur with separation, status loss, and discrimination in a context of unequal power.\cite{link2001} It contributes to population health inequalities by shaping access to material, social, and healthcare resources through structural, interpersonal, and psychological pathways.\cite{hatzenbuehler2013} Clinical documentation is one route through which these pathways operate. Stigmatizing descriptions entered into the record have been shown to worsen provider attitudes and pain-management decisions.\cite{goddu2018} Once recorded, the language stays in the patient's longitudinal record, where it can shape how later clinicians interpret symptoms, judge credibility, and allocate treatment.\cite{goddu2018,park2021} Patients who read their own notes may also encounter it directly, which can undermine their trust and engagement with the healthcare system.\cite{leveille2020notes} The clinical record can therefore pass stigma from one clinician to the next and to patients themselves.
 
%% stigma can be different ... previous ...
Stigmatizing language in clinical records takes multiple forms, including expressions of doubt and disapproval, negative stereotyping, difficult-patient framing, and assertions of clinician authority.\cite{park2021,mcarthur2026} Its prevalence also varies across patient populations: studies have found higher rates of stigmatizing language in notes for patients with substance use disorders, chronic pain, and disability compared with other conditions, as well as disparities by recorded race and ethnicity.\cite{himmelstein2022, sun2022} Clinical documentation is also not written for a single reader. Discharge summaries, for example, contain sections written mainly for clinicians alongside sections written directly for patients.\cite{li2026readability} Existing studies have largely examined clinician-facing narratives without distinguishing whether their findings extend to patient-facing text.

%% introducing section diff
This distinction matters because clinical documentation serves different audiences and functions.\cite{li2026readability} Within a discharge summary, the Brief Hospital Course (BHC) and History of Present Illness (HPI) describe the patient's clinical history and hospital encounter primarily for other clinicians, whereas the Discharge Instructions (DI) communicate information about follow-up care, medications, precautions, and self-care to patients.\cite{xu2024dischargeme,li2026readability} Stigmatizing language in clinician-facing sections may frame subsequent clinical interpretation and influence treatment decisions.\cite{goddu2018} In DI, such language becomes part of direct patient-facing communication, where patients may encounter wording they perceive as judgmental.\cite{leveille2020notes,li2026readability} To our knowledge, prior work has not compared patient-facing DI with clinician-facing sections from the same discharge summary. It therefore remains unclear whether stigmatizing language becomes less common, or takes a different form, when clinicians write directly for patients.

%% intro nlp model used in stigma detection
Natural language processing has made it possible to identify stigmatizing language at scale.\cite{harrigian2023,xue2026,walker2025,scroggins2025} Initial approaches combined domain-informed lexicons with contextual classifiers,\cite{harrigian2023} and later work extended detection through transfer learning, expanded classification frameworks, and domain-specific models.\cite{xue2026,walker2025,scroggins2025} Large language models have also been applied to detecting stigmatizing language and to auditing clinical documentation more generally.\cite{sethi2026,dahal2026} These studies have generally treated the clinical note, or individual sentences within it, as a single undifferentiated unit and have not modeled the intended audience of each section. Prevalence estimates pooled across a document cannot show whether stigmatizing language differs between clinician-facing and patient-facing text.

We therefore compared stigmatizing language in patient-facing DI and clinician-facing BHC and HPI within 259,207 discharge summaries from MIMIC-IV-Note.\cite{mimicnote2023,mimiciv2024} We first quantified the overall difference in stigmatizing language between sections. We then examined whether demographic characteristics and disease status, using diagnosis-code cohorts verified by large language models, were associated with the absolute prevalence in patient-facing DI and with the relative gap between clinician-facing and patient-facing sections.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Overall Pipeline}
The study has three parts, labeled A to C in Figure~\ref{fig:framework}. For each discharge summary, a classifier examined every sentence containing a keyword from a stigma lexicon and labeled it as stigmatizing or non-stigmatizing. The stigmatizing labels were counted separately for each section, and these section-level counts were the study outcome. In parallel, large language models (LLMs) verified the disease cohorts defined by diagnosis codes, checking whether each coded condition was supported by the clinical record. The section counts and cohort membership then entered two statistical models, which estimated how much stigmatizing language differed between sections and whether that difference varied across patient groups.

Part A measures stigmatizing language. The same classifier is applied to every section, so clinician-facing and patient-facing text are judged by one standard, and its labels are summed within each section of each note. Part B defines the sections and patient groups to be compared. Identity groups come from demographic and administrative records, disease cohorts come from diagnosis codes, and LLM verification checks that these disease labels reflect what the note documents; only conditions verified by all five models enter the disease analysis of Model 2. Part C compares sections and groups. Model 1 estimates the overall gap between clinician-facing and patient-facing sections, and Model 2 tests whether that gap is wider or narrower for particular patient groups. After the data sources, each part is described in turn.

\subsection{Data source}
\paragraph{Study cohort}We used discharge summaries from MIMIC-IV-Note version 2.2 (2008--2019), linked to patient demographic, administrative, and diagnosis data from MIMIC-IV version 3.1.\cite{mimicnote2023,mimiciv2024} These deidentified records originate from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center and are publicly available through PhysioNet.\cite{physionet}

We split each summary into sections at curated headers appearing at the start of a line, separating clinician-facing sections (BHC, HPI) from patient-facing text (DI). Of 331,793 summaries, 290,005 contained both a BHC and a DI section. We excluded notes in which either section contained fewer than 50 words, yielding a primary analytic cohort of 259,207 notes with paired BHC and DI sections. HPI was available in 252,581 of these notes and served as a secondary clinician-facing comparison. The admission records of 20 of these notes, present in MIMIC-IV version 2.2, have been removed from version 3.1; these notes could not be linked to patient data and were excluded from the group analyses, leaving 259,187 notes from 125,271 patients. 

\paragraph{Classifier training data}
Training data came from the labeled corpus released by Harrigian and colleagues.\cite{harrigian2023} The corpus contains 5,043 annotated instances, each consisting of a sentence that contains a stigma-relevant keyword and one sentence of context on either side. The keywords come from a curated lexicon of 89 keywords organized into three families: Credibility \& Obstinacy, Compliance, and Descriptors. Each instance was annotated for the type of language use it reflected, and we collapsed these annotations into two categories, stigmatizing and non-stigmatizing, using the mapping described in the Supplementary Methods. 

\subsection{A. Stigma classifier}
We trained a binary classifier that takes a sentence as input and labels it as stigmatizing or non-stigmatizing. Following the design of the Harrigian annotation resource,\cite{harrigian2023} each input sentence contains one keyword from the 89-keyword stigma lexicon and is expanded with one sentence of context on either side, with the keyword marked. We used this classifier to label the keyword-containing sentences in the clinician-facing sections (BHC and HPI) and the patient-facing section (DI) of every discharge summary in the analysis cohort.

To train the classifier, we fine-tuned 12 pretrained language-model encoders on the Harrigian annotations, split by note into training, validation, and test sets of 4,037, 501, and 505 instances. GatorTron-base\cite{yang2022} achieved the highest validation macro-F1 and was selected; the full training configuration and the performance of all 12 models are reported in the Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Table S1. The result is a single sentence-level classifier, which we used to decide whether each keyword sentence in each section was stigmatizing or non-stigmatizing.

\subsection{B. Sections and disease cohorts}

\subsubsection{Section roles}
For this analysis, DI was treated as patient-facing and all other discharge-summary sections as clinician-facing. Section comparisons used BHC as the primary and HPI as the secondary clinician-facing section (Table~\ref{tab:sections}).

\subsubsection{Disease cohorts and LLM verification}
Eight disease cohorts were defined from the ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnosis codes recorded for each admission; an admission belonged to a cohort when any of its codes matched the cohort's code list (Supplementary Table S27). To check these code-based labels against the clinical record, five open-weight LLMs (GLM-4-32B, Gemma-4-31B, Qwen3.5-27B, Mistral-Small-3.2-24B, and gpt-oss-20b) judged whether each admission--disease pair was supported by the past medical history, chief complaint, diagnoses, results, examination, and medications. BHC, DI, HPI, and Social History were withheld from this step so that verification did not draw on the sections in which stigmatizing language was measured, and the ICD codes were not shown to the models. An admission was treated as a case for a condition only if all five models returned SUPPORTED for that admission--disease pair; REFUTED, INSUFFICIENT, and unparseable responses did not count as support. The 40,462 pairs with fewer than five SUPPORTED votes were reclassified as not having the condition rather than excluded, so that each admission continued to contribute to the comparisons for the other conditions. These included 648 pairs in which at least one model returned an unparseable response, 171 of which were supported by every model that responded. Agreement among the models and the effect of the vote threshold are reported in Supplementary Tables S17--S23.

\subsection{C. Statistical models}

\subsubsection{Model 1: section comparison}
To compare how much stigmatizing language each section contains, we counted, in every section of every note, the sentences that the classifier labeled stigmatizing; each such sentence is one stigmatizing-language event. We modeled these counts with generalized estimating equations (GEE), a Poisson regression for counts that accounts for the correlation among sections of the same note.\cite{zeger1986} The model was
\[
\log E(Y_{is})=\log(T_{is})+\beta_0+\beta_1BHC_s+\beta_2HPI_s,
\]
where $Y_{is}$ is the number of events in section $s$ of note $i$ and $T_{is}$ is its number of words. The term $\log(T_{is})$ makes the model describe events per word, so a longer section does not appear more stigmatizing simply because it is longer. $BHC_s$ and $HPI_s$ indicate the BHC and HPI sections, with DI as the reference, so $\beta_0$ is the log event rate in DI. Because the model is on the log scale, exponentiating a coefficient gives a rate ratio (RR): $\exp(\beta_1)$ is the event rate per word in BHC divided by that in DI, and $\exp(\beta_2)$ is the same ratio for HPI. An RR of 8, for example, means that for the same amount of text the event rate in BHC is eight times the rate in DI. The model has no other covariates; estimation details are given in the Supplementary Methods.

To check that the result did not depend on the classifier, we refitted the model with a GatorTron classifier that uses a word-based context window (Word10) and with 10 alternative sentence-window encoders with test accuracy above 0.90, and we checked the direction of the difference with paired Wilcoxon tests, which do not rely on the Poisson model. Full results are reported in the Supplement.

\subsubsection{Model 2: disease and identity modification}
Model 2 asks whether the gap between clinician-facing and patient-facing sections is wider or narrower for particular patient groups. It compares BHC with DI and adds a group indicator and its interaction with section:
\[
\log E(Y_{is})=\log(T_{is})+\beta_0+\beta_1BHC_s+\beta_2G_i+\beta_3(BHC_s\times G_i)+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf Z_i,
\]
where $G_i$ equals 1 for notes from patients in the group, such as a disease cohort, and 0 otherwise, and $\mathbf Z_i$ contains the adjustment variables. As in Model 1, $\exp(\beta_1)$ is the BHC vs DI RR, here for notes outside the group. The interaction term measures how this RR changes within the group: $\exp(\beta_3)$, the interaction RR, is the BHC vs DI RR among notes in the group divided by the BHC vs DI RR among notes outside it. An interaction RR above 1 means a wider gap in the group and below 1 a narrower gap; for age, it is the change in the BHC vs DI RR per additional year. Because an interaction RR is a ratio of two ratios, we report it together with the absolute BHC and DI rates in each group.

Model 2 used the same GEE approach as Model 1 but clustered on patient, because a patient could contribute more than one admission. Model 2 was fitted separately for disease and for identity. The disease analysis included the eight LLM-verified conditions together (substance use disorder, serious mental illness, sickle cell disease, chronic pain, obesity, HIV, end-stage renal disease, and diabetes), each compared with its absence. The identity analysis included documented race and ethnicity, sex, Medicaid status, limited English proficiency, and age, with White, male, non-Medicaid, and English as references. Both adjusted for identity characteristics, disease indicators, Charlson Comorbidity Index, and log length of stay.\cite{charlson1987,quan2005} Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied separately to the eight disease and nine identity interactions.\cite{benjamini1995} Disease-stratified identity refits are reported in the Supplement.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Section rates}

Stigmatizing-language rates were 0.640 in BHC, 0.490 in HPI, and 0.064 in DI per 1,000 words, based on 64,009, 29,196, and 3,565 events, respectively. In Model 1, the BHC vs DI RR was 8.21 (95\% CI, 7.96-8.47; $P<.001$), and the HPI vs DI RR was 7.21 (95\% CI, 6.94-7.50; $P<.001$). In other words, for the same amount of text, the rate of stigmatizing language in BHC was 8.21 times the rate in DI, and the rate in HPI was 7.21 times the rate in DI (Figure~\ref{fig:sections}). Looking at the keywords alone, exposure was also higher in clinician-facing text: clinicians used them 6.55 times as often per word in BHC as in DI, and a keyword in BHC was 1.54 times as likely to be labeled stigmatizing (Supplementary Results).

\subsection{Patient-facing DI and patient-group results}

Across all paired notes, patient-facing DI contained 3,565 stigmatizing-language events in 56,123,304 words. In total, 3,003 of 259,207 notes (1.16\%, about 1 in 86) contained at least one DI event. Repeated events in one DI increase the event rate without increasing the number of DI-positive notes. This prevalence describes occurrence in patient-facing text; patient responses were not available.

LLM verification retained between 51.3\% (chronic pain) and 93.9\% (HIV) of ICD-coded notes in each disease cohort (Table~\ref{tab:groups}; Figure~\ref{fig:disease}). Absolute DI rates varied across the verified cohorts. Relative to the overall DI rate of 0.064, DI rates were highest for sickle cell disease (0.277, 4.3 times the overall rate; 16 events in 377 notes), serious mental illness (0.218, 3.4 times; 328 events in 8,688 notes), and substance use disorder (0.197, 3.1 times; 502 events in 14,060 notes). DI rates were 0.110 for chronic pain, 0.102 for HIV, 0.093 for end-stage renal disease, 0.068 for diabetes, and 0.045 for obesity. These are crude rates among notes in each cohort, whereas the interaction RRs compare the BHC vs DI RR between notes with and without the condition.

The three cohorts with the highest DI rates also had significantly narrower gaps: sickle cell disease (interaction RR, 0.359; 95\% CI, 0.16-0.79; $q=0.018$), serious mental illness (interaction RR, 0.753; 95\% CI, 0.65-0.88; $q=0.001$), and substance use disorder (interaction RR, 0.829; 95\% CI, 0.72-0.96; $q=0.018$). For serious mental illness, an interaction RR of 0.753 means the BHC vs DI RR was about 25\% smaller than in notes without the condition. The sickle cell estimate rests on 16 DI events and has a wide interval. Obesity (interaction RR, 1.329; 95\% CI, 1.11-1.60; $q=0.007$) and diabetes (interaction RR, 1.291; 95\% CI, 1.17-1.43; $q<0.001$) widened the gap. For obesity, the BHC vs DI RR was about 33\% larger than in notes without the condition, although its BHC rate of 0.630 was close to the overall BHC rate of 0.640 and its DI rate of 0.045 was the lowest among the eight cohorts. HIV widened the gap in direction (interaction RR, 1.396; 95\% CI, 0.996-1.96) but did not meet the false discovery threshold ($q=0.070$), and end-stage renal disease and chronic pain showed no modification.

\subsection{Classifier performance and sensitivity analyses}

The fine-tuned sentence-window GatorTron classifier achieved validation macro-F1 of 0.9294 and accuracy of 0.9361; test macro-F1 was 0.9281 and accuracy was 0.9327 (Supplementary Table S1). These held-out metrics describe sentence-level binary classification and do not establish calibration within each section.

The Word10 classifier had higher test macro-F1 and accuracy (0.9429 and 0.9465) than the sentence-window classifier (0.9281 and 0.9327) and was used as a context-window sensitivity analysis.

With the Word10 model, the BHC vs DI RR was 8.26 (95\% CI, 8.01-8.51) and the HPI vs DI RR was 7.29 (95\% CI, 7.01-7.58). Across 10 sentence-window encoders with test accuracy above 0.90, the BHC vs DI RR ranged from 8.09 to 8.79. The Word10 and alternative-encoder estimates were therefore close to the primary RRs of 8.21 and 7.21.

The disease results were also stable across encoders. In the cohorts verified by all five LLMs, obesity and diabetes widened the gap and substance use disorder, serious mental illness, and sickle cell disease narrowed it, each meeting the false discovery threshold under all 10 encoders. HIV widened the gap in direction under all 10 encoders and met the threshold under 6, and end-stage renal disease and chronic pain met it under none. A three-vote majority threshold gave the same pattern (substance use disorder interaction RR, 0.825, $q=0.010$; HIV $q=0.057$; Supplementary Table S23).

Paired Wilcoxon tests agreed with the GEE contrasts in direction (all $P<.001$). All sensitivity estimates retained DI as the reference and section word counts as the denominator. The direction was the same across context windows and encoders.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Principal findings}

Within the same discharge summaries, clinicians wrote far more stigmatizing language in the sections meant for other clinicians than in the instructions meant for patients: for the same amount of text, the rate of stigmatizing language in BHC was 8.21 times the rate in DI (95\% CI, 7.96-8.47; $P<.001$), and the rate in HPI was 7.21 times the rate in DI. Clinicians also used the keywords themselves much more often in clinician-facing narrative. Among disease cohorts, the gap was narrowest for substance use disorder, serious mental illness, and sickle cell disease, whose patient-facing instructions carried the most stigmatizing language, and widest for obesity and diabetes.

\subsection{Clinician-facing and patient-facing text}

Earlier studies showed that stigmatizing language is common in clinical notes, varies across patient groups, and can be identified automatically.\cite{himmelstein2022,harrigian2023,xue2026,walker2025,scroggins2025} These studies analyzed the note as a whole. Comparing the sections of one discharge summary by their intended audience shows that this language sits mainly in the narrative clinicians write for one another. In BHC and HPI it can shape how the next clinician sees the patient.\cite{goddu2018} In DI it reaches the patient directly, and 3,003 notes (1.16\%) contained at least one stigmatizing-language event in their instructions.

Two features of these sections can produce the gap. BHC and HPI reconstruct the admission as a compressed third-person narrative and often describe adherence, credibility, behavior, and interactions with staff, which gives clinicians many occasions for evaluative words. DI is forward-looking and instructional, and clinicians who expect the patient to read it may choose their words more carefully. The keyword data fit both features. Looking at the keywords alone, exposure was much higher in BHC: clinicians used the keywords 6.55 times as often per word in BHC as in DI, and a keyword used in BHC was 1.54 times as likely to be labeled stigmatizing (Supplementary Results).

Because each comparison is made within one admission and one summary, the patient and the hospital stay are the same on both sides of the gap. HPI, a second clinician-facing section, showed a similar gap (HPI vs DI RR, 7.21).

\subsection{Disease cohorts}

The disease results show where patients read the most stigmatizing language. Substance use disorder, serious mental illness, and sickle cell disease had DI rates 3.1 to 4.3 times the all-note rate, and their gaps between BHC and DI were significantly narrower. Their BHC rates were also above the all-note rate, so the narrower gaps came from more stigmatizing language in the instructions these patients received. Earlier work found stigmatizing language concentrated in notes for patients with substance use disorders and showed that it changed clinicians' attitudes and pain management for a patient with sickle cell disease.\cite{himmelstein2022,goddu2018} For these patients, the language also appears in the text written for them.

One explanation is that discharge instructions for these patients more often address substance use, pain control, medication adherence, or crisis plans, topics in which these keywords are common. Obesity showed the opposite pattern: its gap was wider because its DI rate was the lowest of the eight cohorts while its BHC rate was close to the all-note rate, which would follow if instructions for these patients seldom discuss the behaviors the lexicon describes. Examining which keywords occur in DI within each cohort would test these explanations.

These results also show why the interaction RR should be reported with the absolute rates. Here a narrower gap meant that patient-facing text carried more stigmatizing language, and the interaction RRs alone would have hidden the cohorts whose instructions contained the most.

\subsection{Implications for informatics practice}

Stigma surveillance dashboards and documentation-equity audits should keep clinician-facing and patient-facing sections separate. A whole-note rate cannot show whether stigmatizing language occurred in clinician-facing documentation or in patient-facing DI, and it mixes the language within sections with section length. A practical display would report notes, words, stigmatizing-language events, and event rates by section, keep the whole-note rate as a secondary summary, and label absolute rates separately from interaction RRs.

Authoring support should also account for the intended audience of each section. Clinicians use these keywords at different rates in clinician-facing narrative and patient-facing instructions, and the same keyword may be stigmatizing or neutral depending on its context. A section-aware tool should therefore show the full sentence instead of flagging a keyword on its own, and any review threshold should be set with section-specific human labels.

Most discharge summaries had no stigmatizing language in DI, so an alert on every summary would be poorly targeted; retrospective review or sampled auditing fits a prevalence of 1.16\% better. The cohorts with the highest DI rates, substance use disorder, serious mental illness, and sickle cell disease, are natural priorities for that review and for patient co-design.

\subsection{Limitations}

This retrospective analysis used one deidentified corpus from a single academic center, whose templates and documentation practices define the study setting. The classifier covers only expressions containing 89 predefined keywords and excludes open-vocabulary forms from its estimand. Training annotations came mainly from third-person clinical contexts, and section-stratified labels were unavailable, so calibration may differ between clinician-facing and patient-facing sections. Of the 4,710 notes annotated for classifier development, 3,552 (about 1.4\% of the cohort) were also in the analysis cohort, so the classifier scored some passages it had seen during training; excluding them did not change any conclusion (BHC vs DI RR, 8.30; 95\% CI, 8.04-8.57), and all disease-cohort and identity results kept their direction and significance (Supplementary Table S24). Header parsing and the 50-word threshold favor summaries that follow recognizable templates. Within-note pairing holds the patient, admission, and document constant, but section authorship was not observed. Administrative race and ethnicity, sex, language, and insurance fields incompletely represent social identity. Disease cohorts relied on administrative diagnosis codes, and the ICD-9 and ICD-10 code lists were not fully equivalent in scope. ICD-10 F10--F19 include substance-induced disorders, and their use codes may capture prescribed medication, whereas ICD-9 alcohol-induced mental disorders (291) were not included, leaving 202 admissions with these codes outside the substance use disorder cohort. The ICD-9 sickle cell list also excluded 282.64, a sickle cell disease code, which removed 9 admissions from that cohort. Because these admissions were never submitted to LLM verification, they remained in the comparison groups. Charlson scores were computed from all recorded diagnosis codes, including codes for conditions that LLM verification did not support, so the severity adjustment may partly recover a reclassified disease exposure. Coding missing insurance as non-Medicaid and missing language as English may distort group comparisons. Sparse events in some cohorts, residual clinical differences, and the observational design further limit causal interpretation of section and group contrasts. DI is patient-facing, but MIMIC-IV-Note does not contain patient responses; the study therefore does not establish how patients interpreted or were affected by the language.

\section{Conclusion}

Stigmatizing language in discharge summaries differed sharply by the intended audience of each section. For the same amount of text, the rate of stigmatizing language in the clinician-facing BHC was 8.21 times the rate in the patient-facing discharge instructions, and the rate in HPI was 7.21 times that rate. Clinicians also used the keywords far more often in that narrative. Patient-facing instructions still carried stigmatizing language in 1.16\% of notes, most of all for patients with substance use disorder, serious mental illness, and sickle cell disease. Reporting by section separates language that circulates among clinicians from language that patients receive, and keeps changes in document composition from being mistaken for changes in language.
