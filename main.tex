\abstract{\textbf{Objective:} Stigmatizing language in clinical notes can influence clinicians' judgments and treatment decisions, with potential consequences for patient outcomes. Its use may also depend on whom a section of the note is written for. We aimed to quantify differences in stigmatizing language between clinician-facing and patient-facing sections and to examine how these differences varied across patient cohorts.\\
\textbf{Materials and Methods:} We analyzed 259,207 discharge summaries from MIMIC-IV-Note, extracting the clinician-facing Brief Hospital Course (BHC) and History of Present Illness (HPI) sections and the patient-facing Discharge Instructions (DI) section. A fine-tuned language-model classifier labeled keyword-containing sentences as stigmatizing or non-stigmatizing. Disease cohorts defined by diagnosis codes were audited by five large language models, and only conditions confirmed by all five were analyzed. Poisson generalized estimating equation models compared rates of stigmatizing language across sections and examined whether the differences varied across patient cohorts.\\
\textbf{Results:} The stigma classifier achieved a test macro-F1 score of 0.928. Rates of stigmatizing language were 8.21 times higher in BHC than in DI and 7.21 times higher in HPI than in DI. Substance use disorder, serious mental illness, and sickle cell disease had the highest rates in patient-facing instructions and significantly narrower differences between clinician-facing and patient-facing sections, whereas obesity and diabetes widened the difference.\\
\textbf{Discussion:} Stigmatizing language differed sharply by the intended audience of a section. Whole-note estimates combine clinician-facing narrative with patient-facing instructions, which hides where the language appears and how patterns across patient groups differ between the two.\\
\textbf{Conclusion:} Measuring stigmatizing language by section separates what clinicians write for one another from what patients are given to read, and shows where audience-specific documentation review could focus.}

\section{Background and Significance}
%% why stigma is important
Stigma occurs when labeling and stereotyping co-occur with separation, status loss, and discrimination in a context of unequal power.\cite{link2001} It contributes to population health inequalities by shaping access to material, social, and healthcare resources through structural, interpersonal, and psychological pathways.\cite{hatzenbuehler2013} Clinical documentation is one route through which these pathways operate. Stigmatizing descriptions entered into the record have been shown to worsen provider attitudes and pain-management decisions.\cite{goddu2018} Once recorded, the language stays in the patient's longitudinal record, where it can shape how later clinicians interpret symptoms, judge credibility, and allocate treatment.\cite{goddu2018,park2021} Patients who read their own notes may also encounter it directly, which can undermine their trust and engagement with the healthcare system.\cite{leveille2020notes} The clinical record can therefore pass stigma from one clinician to the next and to patients themselves.
 
%% stigma can be different ... previous ...
Stigmatizing language in clinical records takes multiple forms, including expressions of doubt and disapproval, negative stereotyping, difficult-patient framing, and assertions of clinician authority.\cite{park2021,mcarthur2026} Its prevalence also varies across patient populations: studies have found higher rates of stigmatizing language in notes for patients with substance use disorders, chronic pain, and disability compared with other conditions, as well as disparities by recorded race and ethnicity.\cite{himmelstein2022, sun2022} Clinical documentation is also not written for a single reader. Discharge summaries, for example, contain sections written mainly for clinicians alongside sections written directly for patients.\cite{li2026readability} Existing studies have largely examined clinician-facing narratives without distinguishing whether their findings extend to patient-facing text.

%% introducing section diff
This distinction matters because clinical documentation serves different audiences and functions.\cite{li2026readability} Within a discharge summary, the Brief Hospital Course (BHC) and History of Present Illness (HPI) describe the patient's clinical history and hospital encounter primarily for other clinicians, whereas the Discharge Instructions (DI) communicate information about follow-up care, medications, precautions, and self-care to patients.\cite{xu2024dischargeme,li2026readability} Stigmatizing language in clinician-facing sections may frame subsequent clinical interpretation and influence treatment decisions.\cite{goddu2018} In DI, such language becomes part of direct patient-facing communication, where patients may encounter wording they perceive as judgmental.\cite{leveille2020notes,li2026readability} To our knowledge, prior work has not compared patient-facing DI with clinician-facing sections from the same discharge summary. It therefore remains unclear whether stigmatizing language becomes less frequent, or takes a different form, when clinicians write directly for patients.

%% intro nlp model used in stigma detection
Natural language processing has made it possible to identify stigmatizing language at scale.\cite{harrigian2023,xue2026,walker2025,scroggins2025} Initial approaches combined domain-informed lexicons with contextual classifiers,\cite{harrigian2023} and later work extended detection through transfer learning, expanded classification frameworks, and domain-specific models.\cite{xue2026,walker2025,scroggins2025} Large language models have also been applied to detecting stigmatizing language and to auditing clinical documentation more generally.\cite{sethi2026,dahal2026} These studies have generally treated the clinical note, or individual sentences within it, as a single undifferentiated unit and have not modeled the intended audience of each section. Prevalence estimates pooled across a document cannot show whether stigmatizing language differs between clinician-facing and patient-facing text.

We therefore compared stigmatizing language in patient-facing DI and clinician-facing BHC and HPI within 259,207 discharge summaries from MIMIC-IV-Note.\cite{mimicnote2023,mimiciv2024} We first quantified the overall difference in stigmatizing language between sections. We then examined whether demographic characteristics and disease status, using diagnosis-code cohorts confirmed by large language model review, were associated with the absolute prevalence in patient-facing DI and with the relative gap between clinician-facing and patient-facing sections.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Overall Pipeline}
The study has three parts (Figure~\ref{fig:framework}). For each discharge summary, a classifier examined every sentence containing a term from a stigma lexicon and labeled it as stigmatizing or non-stigmatizing. The stigmatizing labels were counted separately for each section, and these section-level counts were the study outcome. In parallel, large language models (LLMs) audited the patient cohorts defined by diagnosis codes, checking whether each coded condition was supported by the clinical record. The section counts and cohort membership then entered two statistical models, which estimated how much stigmatizing language differed between sections and whether that difference varied across patient groups.

The first part measures stigmatizing language. The same classifier is applied to every section, so clinician-facing and patient-facing text are judged by one standard, and its labels are summed within each section of each note. The second part defines the patient groups to be compared. Identity groups come from demographic and administrative records, disease cohorts come from diagnosis codes, and the LLM audit checks that these disease labels reflect what the note documents; only conditions confirmed by all five models enter the disparity models. The third part compares sections and groups. The first model estimates the overall gap between clinician-facing and patient-facing sections, and the second tests whether that gap is wider or narrower for particular patient groups. Each part is described in the subsections that follow.

\subsection{Data source}
\paragraph{Study cohort}We used discharge summaries from MIMIC-IV-Note version 2.2 (2008--2019), linked to patient demographic, administrative, and diagnosis data from MIMIC-IV version 3.1.\cite{mimicnote2023,mimiciv2024} These deidentified records originate from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center and are publicly available through PhysioNet.\cite{physionet}

We split each summary into sections at curated headers appearing at the start of a line, separating clinician-facing sections (BHC, HPI) from patient-facing text (DI). Of 331,793 summaries, 290,005 contained both a BHC and a DI section. We excluded notes in which either section contained fewer than 50 words, yielding a primary analytic cohort of 259,207 notes with paired BHC and DI sections. HPI was available in 252,581 of these notes and served as a secondary clinician-facing comparison. The admission records of 20 of these notes, present in MIMIC-IV version 2.2, have been removed from version 3.1; these notes could not be linked to patient data and were excluded from the group analyses, leaving 259,187 notes from 125,271 patients. 

\paragraph{Disease cohorts and LLM audit}
Eight disease cohorts were defined from the ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnosis codes recorded for each admission; an admission belonged to a cohort when any of its codes matched the cohort's code list (Supplementary Table S26). To check these code-based labels against the clinical record, five open-weight LLMs judged whether each admission--disease pair was supported by the past medical history, chief complaint, diagnoses, results, examination, and medications. BHC, DI, HPI, and Social History were withheld from this step so that the audit did not draw on the sections in which stigmatizing language was measured, and the ICD codes were not shown to the models. An admission was treated as a case for a condition only if all five models returned SUPPORTED for that admission--disease pair; REFUTED, INSUFFICIENT, and unparseable responses did not count as support. The 40,462 pairs with fewer than five SUPPORTED votes were reclassified as not having the condition rather than excluded, so that each admission continued to contribute to the comparisons for the other conditions. These included 648 pairs in which at least one model returned an unparseable response, 171 of which were supported by every model that responded. Agreement among the models and the effect of the vote threshold are reported in Supplementary Tables S16--S22.

\paragraph{Classifier training data}
Training data came from the labeled corpus released by Harrigian and colleagues.\cite{harrigian2023} The corpus contains 5,043 annotated instances, each consisting of a sentence that contains a stigma-relevant keyword and one sentence of context on either side. The keywords come from a curated lexicon of 89 terms organized into three families: Credibility \& Obstinacy, Compliance, and Descriptors. Each instance was annotated for the type of language use it reflected, and we collapsed these annotations into two categories, stigmatizing and non-stigmatizing, using the mapping described in the Supplementary Methods.

\subsection{Stigma classifier}

We trained a binary classifier that takes a sentence as input and labels it as stigmatizing or non-stigmatizing. Following the design of the Harrigian annotation resource,\cite{harrigian2023} each input sentence contains one keyword from the 89-term stigma lexicon and is expanded with one sentence of context on either side, with the keyword marked. We used this classifier to label the keyword-containing sentences in the clinician-facing sections (BHC and HPI) and the patient-facing section (DI) of every discharge summary in the analysis cohort.

To train the classifier, we fine-tuned 12 pretrained language-model encoders on the Harrigian annotations, split by note into training, validation, and test sets of 4,037, 501, and 505 instances. GatorTron-base\cite{yang2022} achieved the highest validation macro-F1 and was selected; the full training configuration and the performance of all 12 models are reported in the Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Table S1. The result is a single sentence-level classifier, which we used to decide whether each keyword sentence in each section was stigmatizing or non-stigmatizing.

Two further descriptive analyses addressed specific alternative explanations. \subsection{Identity and disease modification}

These models ask whether the difference between clinician-facing and patient-facing sections is larger or smaller for particular patient groups, defined by identity characteristics and by disease. For a group indicator $G_i$, the common model was
\[
\begin{aligned}
\log E(Y_{is})={}&\log(T_{is})+\beta_0+\beta_1BHC_s+\beta_2G_i\\
&+\beta_3(BHC_s\times G_i)+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf Z_i,
\end{aligned}
\]
where $\mathbf Z_i$ contains the adjustment variables. With DI and $G_i=0$ as references, $\exp(\beta_1)$ is the BHC to DI RR in the reference group, whereas $\exp(\beta_2)$ compares $G_i=1$ with $G_i=0$ within DI. The interaction is a ratio of rate ratios:
\[
\exp(\beta_3)=
\frac{R_{BHC,G=1}/R_{DI,G=1}}{R_{BHC,G=0}/R_{DI,G=0}}.
\]
Here $R$ denotes a model-implied section rate at common covariate values. Values above 1 denote a wider relative section gap; values below 1 denote a narrower gap. Under the model's log-linear age specification, $\exp(\beta_3)$ is the multiplicative change in the BHC to DI RR per additional year. Because this coefficient is a ratio of ratios, we report it alongside absolute BHC and DI rates to show which section drives the difference.

These models used the same Poisson GEE approach as the section comparison, with section word counts as the denominator, but clustered on patient rather than note because a patient could contribute more than one admission and summary\td{what are group models? section-based models are group models?}\td{this section somehow comes a bit sudden and not sure how this links to your method.}. They used an exchangeable working correlation, Pearson scale, and robust covariance. Identity models jointly interacted section with documented race and ethnicity, documented sex, Medicaid status, limited English proficiency, and sample-centered age. White, male, non-Medicaid, and English categories were the respective references. Disease models jointly interacted section with LLM-verified substance use disorder, serious mental illness, sickle cell disease, chronic pain, obesity, HIV, end-stage renal disease, and diabetes, with absence of each condition as the reference. Both model families adjusted for identity, disease indicators, Charlson Comorbidity Index, and log length of stay.\cite{charlson1987,quan2005} Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied within the nine identity and eight disease interaction families.\cite{benjamini1995} Disease-stratified identity refits and sparse-cell eligibility rules are reported in the Supplement.


\section{Results}

\subsection{Section rates and decomposition}

Stigmatizing-language rates were 0.640 in BHC, 0.490 in HPI, and 0.064 in DI per 1,000 words, based on 64,009, 29,196, and 3,565 events, respectively. The GEE-estimated BHC-DI RR was 8.210 (95\% CI, 7.963-8.465; $P<.001$), and the HPI-DI RR was 7.212 (95\% CI, 6.937-7.497; $P<.001$). In other words, for the same amount of text, BHC contained about eight times as many stigmatizing-language events as DI, and HPI about seven times as many (Figure~\ref{fig:sections}).

Successfully scored keyword anchors occurred at rates of 1.005 in BHC, 0.765 in HPI, and 0.153 in DI. The crude BHC to DI RR of 10.08 equaled a 6.55-fold anchor-exposure component multiplied by a 1.54-fold conditional component (10.08 = 6.55 $\times$ 1.54; Table~\ref{tab:decomposition}). Put simply, BHC contained 6.55 times as many keyword anchors per word as DI, and each anchor in BHC was 1.54 times as likely to be classified as stigmatizing. For HPI versus DI, the crude RR was 7.72 = 4.98 $\times$ 1.55. The corresponding conditional probabilities were 0.637 in BHC, 0.641 in HPI, and 0.414 in DI (descriptive 95\% Wilson intervals, 0.634-0.640, 0.637-0.646, and 0.404-0.424; inference for section contrasts comes from the paired GEE). BHC and HPI differed in anchor exposure but had similar conditional point estimates, whereas DI had a lower conditional probability within the anchor lexicon.

\subsection{Patient-facing DI and patient-group results}

Across all paired notes, patient-facing DI contained 3,565 stigmatizing-language events in 56,123,304 words. In total, 3,003 of 259,207 notes (1.16\%, about 1 in 86) contained at least one DI event. Repeated events in one DI increase the event rate without increasing the number of DI-positive notes. This prevalence describes occurrence in patient-facing text; patient responses were not available.

LLM confirmation retained between 51.3\% (chronic pain) and 93.9\% (HIV) of ICD-coded notes in each disease cohort (Table~\ref{tab:groups}; Figure~\ref{fig:disease}). Absolute DI rates varied across the confirmed cohorts. Relative to the overall DI rate of 0.064, DI rates were highest for sickle cell disease (0.277, 4.3 times the overall rate; 16 events in 377 notes), serious mental illness (0.218, 3.4 times; 328 events in 8,688 notes), and substance use disorder (0.197, 3.1 times; 502 events in 14,060 notes). DI rates were 0.110 for chronic pain, 0.102 for HIV, 0.093 for end-stage renal disease, 0.068 for diabetes, and 0.045 for obesity. These are crude rates among notes in each cohort, whereas the interaction estimates compare the adjusted relative BHC-DI gap between notes with and without the condition.

The three cohorts with the highest DI rates also had significantly narrower adjusted relative gaps: sickle cell disease (RR, 0.359; 95\% CI, 0.16-0.79; $q=0.018$), serious mental illness (RR, 0.753; 95\% CI, 0.65-0.88; $q=0.001$), and substance use disorder (RR, 0.829; 95\% CI, 0.72-0.96; $q=0.018$). For serious mental illness, an interaction RR of 0.753 means the BHC to DI ratio was about 25\% smaller than in notes without the condition. The sickle cell estimate rests on 16 DI events and has a wide interval. Obesity (RR, 1.329; 95\% CI, 1.11-1.60; $q=0.007$) and diabetes (RR, 1.291; 95\% CI, 1.17-1.43; $q<0.001$) widened the gap. For obesity, the BHC to DI ratio was about 33\% larger than in notes without the condition, although its BHC rate of 0.630 was close to the overall BHC rate of 0.640 and its DI rate of 0.045 was the lowest among the eight cohorts. \subsection{Classifier performance and sensitivity analyses}

The fine-tuned sentence-window GatorTron classifier achieved validation macro-F1 of 0.9294 and accuracy of 0.9361; test macro-F1 was 0.9281 and accuracy was 0.9327 (Supplementary Table S1). These held-out metrics describe candidate-level binary classification and do not establish calibration within each section.

The Word10 classifier had higher test macro-F1 and accuracy (0.9429 and 0.9465) than the sentence-window classifier (0.9281 and 0.9327) and was used as a context-window sensitivity analysis.

The Word10 model produced GEE RRs of 8.26 (95\% CI, 8.01-8.51) for BHC versus DI and 7.29 (95\% CI, 7.01-7.58) for HPI versus DI. Unfiltered keyword-anchor RRs were 5.89 (95\% CI, 5.76-6.02) and 4.79 (95\% CI, 4.67-4.91), respectively. Across 10 sentence-window encoders with test accuracy above 0.90, the BHC to DI RR ranged from 8.09 to 8.79. The Word10 and alternative-encoder estimates were therefore close to the primary RRs of 8.210 and 7.212, whereas counting every anchor without classification gave smaller ratios.

The disease results were also stable across encoders. In the cohorts confirmed by all five LLMs, obesity and diabetes widened the gap and substance use disorder, serious mental illness, and sickle cell disease narrowed it, each meeting the false discovery threshold under all 10 encoders. HIV widened the gap in direction under all 10 encoders and met the threshold under 6, and end-stage renal disease and chronic pain met it under none. A three-vote majority threshold gave the same pattern (substance use disorder RR, 0.825, $q=0.010$; HIV $q=0.057$; Supplementary Table S22).

Paired Wilcoxon tests agreed with the GEE contrasts in direction (all $P<.001$). All sensitivity estimates retained DI as the reference and section word counts as the denominator. Contextual classification changed the size of the contrasts relative to unfiltered anchors, and the direction was the same across context windows and qualifying encoders.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Principal findings}

Stigmatizing language varied sharply across the audience boundary within the same discharge summary. The crude rate was about ten times higher in clinician-facing BHC than in patient-facing DI and 7.72 times higher in clinician-facing HPI than in DI. Most of each contrast reflected how often keyword anchors appeared, and a smaller part remained after conditioning on an anchor. A lower patient-facing rate is not the same as absence: DI contained stigmatizing-language events in 3,003 notes. Among disease cohorts, the conditions with the highest patient-facing rates (sickle cell disease, serious mental illness, and substance use disorder) had narrower gaps between clinician-facing and patient-facing sections, whereas obesity and diabetes had wider gaps.

\subsection{Interpretation}

Earlier studies established that stigma occurs in clinical records, varies across patient populations, and can be detected at scale.\cite{himmelstein2022,harrigian2023,xue2026,walker2025,scroggins2025} This study adds a comparison across the audience boundary within inpatient discharge summaries, setting patient-facing DI against clinician-facing documentation from the same note in paired, length-normalized analyses.

Where the language appears matters because the sections have different readers. In clinician-facing BHC and HPI, stigmatizing language can frame the patient for later clinicians. In patient-facing DI, it appears in instructions that guide the patient's actions after discharge. The much lower DI rate means that, within the same summaries, less of this language appeared in patient-facing communication than in clinician-facing narrative. The analysis did not measure intention, clinical decisions, patient interpretation, or harm; it shows which setting contained the language and where validation or review should focus.

Audience-sensitive writing is one plausible explanation for this pattern. Because DI is written for patients to read and act on, clinicians may expect patients to read it and choose their wording more carefully. DI is also usually forward-looking and instructional, focused on medications, follow-up, precautions, and self-care. Clinician-facing BHC and HPI instead reconstruct the encounter as a compressed third-person narrative. These sections more often describe adherence, credibility, behavior, and interactions with staff, which creates more occasions for evaluative wording and for behavior in a specific situation to be condensed into short descriptors that carry into clinical handoff.

The decomposition is consistent with both explanations. The 6.55-fold BHC anchor-exposure component accounted for most of the crude difference between clinician-facing and patient-facing text, consistent with clinician-facing narrative offering more occasions to describe patient behavior. DI also had a lower probability of stigma conditional on an anchor. This smaller conditional difference is consistent with more careful wording for a patient audience, but it does not demonstrate clinicians' intentions. Direct standardization showed that the conditional contrast persisted after equalizing the three broad anchor families: the BHC-DI ratio was 1.48 and the HPI-DI ratio was 1.44. The standardized comparison still combines exact keyword mix, contextual use, and classifier behavior; it does not isolate a causal effect of audience.

Because BHC and DI come from the same admission and summary, pairing removes between-admission differences from the audience comparison. The sections still differ in content, timing, conventional voice, and possibly author. The contrast therefore describes how stigmatizing language is distributed between clinician-facing and patient-facing documentation without isolating audience as its cause. HPI, a second clinician-facing section, showed a similar conditional point estimate, which supports the pattern, although no equivalence test was performed.

The word totals show why the choice of aggregation matters. BHC made up 64.0\% of combined BHC-DI words, and the observed mix yielded a composite rate 23.0\% higher than equal word weights at fixed section rates. The per-note BHC share varied widely, with a 5th-to-95th percentile range of 26.1\% to 86.7\%. These quantities describe composition, not a causal template effect. A whole-note dashboard can therefore change because the language within sections changed, because document composition changed, or both.

The disease results show why absolute section rates and modification of the relative gap need to be read together. The three conditions with significantly narrower gaps, substance use disorder, serious mental illness, and sickle cell disease, also had the highest DI rates, 3.1 to 4.3 times the all-note rate. Their BHC rates were also above the all-note rate, so the narrower gaps arose from elevated DI rates. Obesity had a wider gap, although its BHC rate was close to the all-note rate and its DI rate was the lowest of the eight cohorts. Reporting only the interaction estimates would therefore hide the cohorts in which patient-facing text carried the most stigmatizing language.

\hy{New interpretation drafted in response to Ting's comment on explaining the results; authors to confirm or revise.}The disease patterns suggest explanations that this study did not test. Discharge instructions for patients with substance use disorder, serious mental illness, or sickle cell disease may more often address topics such as substance use, pain control, medication adherence, or crisis plans, where lexicon terms are more likely to appear; this would raise the DI rate and narrow the relative gap. For obesity, the wider gap reflected a very low DI rate rather than a high BHC rate, which could arise if instructions for these patients seldom discuss the behaviors that the lexicon describes. Examining which anchor terms occur in DI within each cohort would test these explanations.

Equity reporting needs both quantities because they use different comparisons. The interaction compares adjusted relative BHC-DI gaps between an index and reference group, whereas the crude section rate describes stigmatizing-language events per word within the index group. A null interaction can therefore coexist with elevated crude rates in both sections. Stating the denominator, index population, and reference for each estimate keeps a null relative-gap result from being read as evidence of equal absolute rates.

The DI result is a direct reason to keep section identity in such analyses, because DI is the text patients receive. Evaluating how patients interpreted this language, and whether it affected trust, comprehension, or harm, requires patient-level outcome data.

\subsection{Implications for informatics practice}

Stigma surveillance dashboards and documentation-equity audits should keep clinician-facing and patient-facing sections separate. A whole-note rate cannot show whether stigmatizing language occurred in clinician-facing documentation or in patient-facing DI, and it mixes the language within sections with section length. A practical display would report notes, words, stigmatizing-language events, and event rates by section, retain the whole-note rate as a secondary summary, and label absolute rates separately from interaction estimates. Analysts could then see who the text was written for and whether an apparent change reflects documentation structure or the language within a section.

Authoring support should also account for section audience. Generic whole-note alerts ignore differences in anchor opportunity and contextual use between clinician-facing narrative and patient-facing instructions. A section-aware tool should show the full sentence context instead of flagging a term on its own, because the same term may be stigmatizing or neutral depending on context, and these patterns vary by section. Any review threshold would require section-stratified human labels before deployment.

Stigmatizing-language events occurred in DI in 1.16\% of paired notes, which sets the scale for review of patient-facing text. An alert attached to every discharge summary would be poorly targeted because most notes had no DI event. Targeted retrospective review or sampled auditing is more consistent with the observed prevalence, although prospective workflow effects were not evaluated. The high DI rates for substance use disorder and serious mental illness, and the sparse sickle cell estimate, point to priorities for qualitative validation and patient co-design; they do not justify automated judgments about clinicians or patients.

\subsection{Limitations}

This retrospective analysis used one deidentified corpus from a single academic center, whose templates and documentation practices define the study setting. The classifier covers only expressions containing 89 predefined terms and excludes open-vocabulary forms from its estimand. Training annotations came mainly from third-person clinical contexts, and section-stratified labels were unavailable, so calibration may differ between clinician-facing and patient-facing sections. Of the 4,710 notes annotated for classifier development, 3,552 (about 1.4\% of the cohort) were also in the analysis cohort, so the classifier scored some passages it had seen during training; excluding them did not change any conclusion (BHC to DI RR, 8.30; 95\% CI, 8.04-8.57), and all disease-cohort and identity results kept their direction and significance (Supplementary Table S23). Header parsing and the 50-word threshold favor summaries that follow recognizable templates. Within-note pairing holds the patient, admission, and document constant, but section authorship was not observed. Broad-family standardization does not control the exact keyword mix or section-specific classifier error. Administrative race and ethnicity, sex, language, and insurance fields incompletely represent social identity. Disease cohorts relied on administrative diagnosis codes, and the ICD-9 and ICD-10 code lists were not fully equivalent in scope. ICD-10 F10--F19 include substance-induced disorders, and their use codes may capture prescribed medication, whereas ICD-9 alcohol-induced mental disorders (291) were not included, leaving 202 admissions with these codes outside the substance use disorder cohort. The ICD-9 sickle cell list also excluded 282.64, a sickle cell disease code, which removed 9 admissions from that cohort. Because these admissions were never submitted to the LLM audit, they remained in the comparison groups. Charlson scores were computed from all recorded diagnosis codes, including codes for conditions that the LLM audit did not confirm, so the severity adjustment may partly recover a reclassified disease exposure. Coding missing insurance as non-Medicaid and missing language as English may distort group comparisons. Sparse events in some cohorts, residual clinical differences, and the observational design further limit causal interpretation of section and group contrasts. DI is patient-facing, but MIMIC-IV-Note does not contain patient responses; the study therefore does not establish how patients interpreted or were affected by the language.

\section{Conclusion}

Stigmatizing language in discharge summaries differed sharply by the intended audience of each section. Rates were substantially higher in clinician-facing hospital course and history sections than in patient-facing DI, and most of the difference reflected the greater number of keyword-anchor opportunities in clinician-facing narrative. Stigmatizing-language events nevertheless occurred in patient-facing DI in 1.16\% of notes, with higher absolute DI rates in some disease cohorts. Reporting by section separates language that circulates among clinicians from language that patients receive in their instructions, and keeps changes in document composition from being mistaken for changes in language. The results come from a single corpus, MIMIC-IV, and do not establish a causal effect of audience, how patients responded, or classifier performance in other settings.
