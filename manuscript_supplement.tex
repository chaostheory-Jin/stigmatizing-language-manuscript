\section{Supplementary Methods}

\subsection{Section parsing and cohort construction}

Discharge Instructions (DI) was defined as the patient-facing section, while the remaining discharge-summary sections were classified as clinician-facing. The empirical analyses compared DI with Brief Hospital Course (BHC) as the primary clinician-facing section and History of Present Illness (HPI) as a secondary clinician-facing section. Discharge summaries were segmented at curated headers occurring at the start of a line, with adjacent headers delimiting a section body. The primary cohort required both BHC and DI, with at least 50 words in each. HPI was included only in its separate paired comparison. The parser scanned 331,793 notes, found 290,005 with both primary sections, and retained 259,207 after the length threshold. Header rules, the definition of a word, and the full cohort flow are given in the Supplementary Note (Section~\ref{sec:s_processing}).

The group-analysis build linked every retained note to note-level subject and admission identifiers. Twenty notes had no matching patient or admission record because their admissions, present in MIMIC-IV version 2.2, have been removed from version 3.1; they lacked sex and age, and complete-case modeling excluded them. The final group-analysis cohort contained 259,187 notes, 259,187 admissions, 125,271 subjects, and 518,374 paired BHC and DI rows. Missing insurance was coded as non-Medicaid for 2,793 notes. Missing language was coded as English or LEP=0 for 398 notes. These were coding decisions rather than row exclusions.

\subsection{LLM verification of ICD-coded disease cohorts}

Disease cohorts were defined from ICD diagnosis codes linked to each admission. Because a diagnosis code does not guarantee that the condition is documented in the discharge summary, we assessed whether each coded condition was supported by the note text. Each admission--disease pair was adjudicated independently by five open-weight large language models (LLMs): Qwen3.5-27B, Gemma-4-31B, GLM-4-32B, Mistral-24B, and gpt-oss-20b. Each model read seven sections of the discharge summary, with BHC, DI, HPI, Social History, and the ICD codes withheld (Supplementary Note), and returned one of three labels: SUPPORTED, REFUTED, or INSUFFICIENT. The adjudication covered 164,770 admission--disease pairs from 122,301 admissions across eight disease cohorts, for 823,850 model inferences. Outputs that could not be parsed were treated as missing votes rather than as a label: 568 for gpt-oss-20b (0.345\%), 67 for GLM-4-32B (0.041\%), 15 for Mistral-24B (0.009\%), 1 for Gemma-4-31B (0.001\%), and none for Qwen3.5-27B, totaling 651 (0.079\%). All missing verdicts arose from outputs that reached the maximum-token limit before the JSON object closed. Because of an infrastructure fault, 11,193 Qwen3.5-27B pairs were run twice; the first output was kept, and 11,191 of the duplicated pairs (99.98\%) had identical verdicts. The evidence sections, disease definitions, prompt, model versions, and decoding settings are given in the Supplementary Note (Section~\ref{sec:s_llm_protocol}).

Agreement was summarized for the three labels and for a binary collapse of SUPPORTED versus REFUTED or INSUFFICIENT. Overall agreement across the five models used Fleiss \(\kappa\), Krippendorff \(\alpha\), which accommodates missing ratings, and the proportion of pairs on which all five models agreed. Pairwise agreement was calculated for each of the 10 model pairs. Results were stratified by disease and by the position of the qualifying code in the admission's diagnosis sequence: principal (sequence number 1), middle (2--9), and late (10 or higher). In the primary analysis, a pair was verified only when five valid votes were cast and all five were SUPPORTED; REFUTED and INSUFFICIENT counted as not supporting. Unverified pairs were recoded as not having the condition, changing only that disease's indicator, so each admission remained a case for its verified conditions and a control for the others. This recoded 40,462 of the 164,770 pairs, including all 648 pairs with fewer than five valid votes; 171 of these were supported by every model that responded and were recoded only because one model's output could not be parsed. As sensitivity analyses, we refitted the Layer 3 model with the unverified ICD-coded cohorts and with a majority threshold of at least three SUPPORTED votes.

\subsection{Training data}

Classifier training used the Harrigian annotation resource and 89-keyword lexicon. The binary target combined negative, disbelief, and difficult annotations as stigma; positive, neutral, and exclude annotations formed non-stigma. The 5,043 examples were split by note identifier into 4,037 training, 501 validation, and 505 test examples. Primary input contained the keyword-bearing sentence and one adjacent sentence on either side.

\subsection{Stigma classifier and candidate scoring}

We fine-tuned GatorTron-base as the primary binary stigma classifier and selected it by validation macro-F1 from 12 sentence-window encoders. Training used AdamW, an initial learning rate of \(2\times10^{-5}\), a linear scheduler, training and evaluation batch sizes of 16 and 32, 20 epochs, no class weights, BertTokenizer, and a maximum sequence length of 256. The selected checkpoint was epoch 12, step 3036. Training used the TrainingArguments default seed of 42, PyTorch 2.6.0+cu124, and Transformers 4.56.2. Because the model and new classification head were instantiated before the Trainer applied its seed, the saved weights are the authoritative artifact and exact weight reproduction from seed 42 alone is not guaranteed. The upstream base-model revision was not pinned.

In the analysis corpus, the 89-keyword lexicon identified Credibility \& Obstinacy, Compliance, and Descriptors candidates using case-insensitive matching with word boundaries. Candidate generation yielded 154,984 spans. Cleaning, normalization, and relocation within sentence context succeeded for 154,895 candidates. The remaining 89 records failed relocation; none failed after relocation.

For the anchor-family analysis, we streamed the saved binary output and aggregated successfully relocated candidates and stigmatizing-language events by section and the three prespecified broad families. Conditional probabilities use scored candidates as the denominator; the 89 relocation failures were retained in a separate audit. Wilson intervals describe each family-specific probability. For BHC vs DI and HPI vs DI, direct standardization applied the candidate-count distribution pooled across the two compared sections to both sets of family-specific probabilities. We also used the symmetric Kitagawa two-component identity to partition the crude conditional-probability difference into broad-family composition and within-family components. These descriptive components are not causal and do not standardize the exact terms within each family.

For composition, we calculated each section's share of the relevant section word total within every note. We summarized BHC among BHC plus DI, HPI among HPI plus DI, and each of BHC, HPI, and DI among notes containing all three sections.

A note-level rate obeys the identity
\[
R_{note}=\sum_s w_sR_s, \qquad w_s=\frac{T_s}{\sum_j T_j},
\]
where $R_s$ is the stigmatizing-language rate and $T_s$ the word count in section $s$. Section composition therefore enters every note-level rate that ignores sections through the word weights $w_s$: with the section-specific rates $R_s$ held fixed, the note-level rate still changes when the word shares $w_s$ change.

\subsection{Section and group-modification models}

The section comparison used a Poisson generalized estimating equation (GEE), log word-count offset, exchangeable working correlation, note-level clustering, and robust sandwich standard errors. DI was the reference. Paired Wilcoxon tests provided a directional check. GEE targets a population-average rate ratio; because the exchangeable working correlation weights notes differently from a pooled total, GEE rate ratios differ from crude ratios of corpus totals (8.21 versus 10.08 for the BHC vs DI RR). The word-count offset fixes the coefficient on the log word count at 1, so expected event counts are compared at a common amount of text. Robust standard errors keep inference valid if the exchangeable working correlation is misspecified, although they do not correct outcome misclassification by the language model. In both models the identity covariates were documented race and ethnicity, sex, Medicaid status, limited English proficiency, and age centered at the sample mean; under the log-linear age term, the age interaction RR is the multiplicative change in the BHC vs DI RR per additional year.

All group-modification models used a Poisson log link, offset \(\log(\text{word count})\), subject-level clustering, exchangeable working correlation, Pearson \(X^2\) scale, robust covariance, and at most 100 iterations. The target term was the section by group interaction. An interaction RR greater than 1 indicates a wider BHC vs DI RR than the reference.

Layer 1 used:
\[
\begin{split}
n\_stigma \sim section \times (&race + sex + medicaid + lep + age_c + sud + smi\\
&+ sicklecell + chronicpain + obesity + hiv + esrd + diabetes\\
&+ charlson_c + log\_los).
\end{split}
\]
The nine identity interactions formed one BH-FDR family. Layer 2 refitted identity models within cohorts with at least 1,000 notes. Each disease cohort formed a separate BH-FDR family. Levels required at least 50 notes, 20 BHC events, and 5 DI events. Post-fit checks required convergence, finite estimates, \(0.02<RR<50\), and a CI ratio below 200. Layer 2 provides disease-stratified identity estimates.

Layer 3 jointly interacted section with all eight disease indicators and with identity and severity covariates. In the primary analysis the disease indicators were those verified by all five LLMs; Table~\ref{tab:s_layer3} reports the same model with the unverified ICD-coded indicators. Its eight disease interactions formed one BH-FDR family.

Age diagnostics used the 259,187-note complete-case group-analysis cohort. Crude age-stratified absolute rates used bands of 18--39, 40--64, 65--79, and 80 years or older, exact Poisson intervals for each rate, and log-scale intervals for crude BHC vs DI RRs. The nonlinear diagnostic retained the primary Poisson GEE structure, covariates, subject clustering, log word-count offset, exchangeable working correlation, Pearson scale, and robust covariance. Age was represented by a restricted cubic spline with knots at 28, 56, 70, and 89 years, corresponding to the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles. A joint robust Wald test evaluated the two section-by-nonlinear-basis terms. Delta-method intervals accompany the BHC vs DI gap at each age relative to the fitted gap at the sample mean age of 61.8 years.

\subsection{Sensitivity analyses}

The Word10 binary GatorTron model provided context-window sensitivity. The regenerated Word10 input used the official cleaned and normalized coordinate system, with exact keyword-offset agreement in all 5,043 examples. Ten of 12 sentence-window binary encoders with test accuracy above 0.90 provided encoder sensitivity for the section comparison. Full validation and test performance for all sentence-window and Word10 binary encoders is reported in Supplementary Table S1.

\subsection{Decomposition of the section contrast}

A higher rate in BHC could arise because clinician-facing text contains more keyword anchors (occurrences of lexicon keywords), or because anchors there are more often used in a stigmatizing way. To separate these explanations, we decomposed each crude section RR into an anchor-exposure component and a conditional component:
\[
\frac{R_A}{R_{DI}} =
\frac{K_A}{K_{DI}}
\times
\frac{R_A/K_A}{R_{DI}/K_{DI}},
\]
where $A$ is BHC or HPI, $R_s$ is the predicted stigmatizing-language rate, and $K_s$ is the rate of scored keyword anchors in section $s$. The anchor-exposure component $K_A/K_{DI}$ measures how often a section offers a keyword-defined occasion to describe patient behavior. The conditional component compares $P(\mathrm{predicted\ stigma}\mid\mathrm{scored\ anchor})=R_s/K_s$ within a fixed anchor lexicon. In words, the crude section RR equals the relative frequency of scored anchors multiplied by the relative probability that an anchor is classified as stigmatizing. This decomposition is descriptive: it uses exact event, scored-candidate, and word counts and is separate from the adjusted GEE estimates.

Two further descriptive analyses addressed specific alternative explanations. To ask whether DI's lower conditional probability simply reflected a different mix of anchor types, we tabulated scored candidates, events, and Wilson intervals by section and broad anchor family and standardized the conditional probabilities to a common family distribution. To ask how much a whole-note rate, as used in prior studies, depends on section length alone, we summarized each section's share of the words in a note using means, standard deviations, medians, interquartile ranges, and 5th and 95th percentiles.

\subsection{LLM verification of disease cohorts}

Votes were strongly polarized (Table~\ref{tab:s_llm_votes}). All five models labeled 124,308 admission--disease pairs (75.44\%) as SUPPORTED, and none did for 23,834 pairs (14.47\%); together these unanimous outcomes covered 89.91\% of pairs. Only 8,499 pairs (5.16\%) received two or three SUPPORTED votes, so the choice of vote threshold affects a small share of the cohort. All five models assigned the same three-class label to 146,100 pairs (88.67\%).

Overall agreement was almost perfect by Landis and Koch benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:s_llm_overall}): Fleiss \(\kappa\) was 0.816 for three classes and 0.838 for the binary outcome, and Krippendorff \(\alpha\) was 0.817 and 0.839. Because \(\alpha\) accommodates missing ratings and differed from \(\kappa\) by less than 0.001, the 651 unparsed outputs did not materially affect agreement. Binary pairwise \(\kappa\) ranged from 0.770 to 0.913, with a median of 0.841, and all 10 pairs exceeded 0.60 (Table~\ref{tab:s_llm_pairwise}).

Models differed in how readily they assigned SUPPORTED (Table~\ref{tab:s_llm_models}). GLM-4-32B was the most permissive (83.89\%) and had the lowest mean pairwise \(\kappa\) (0.814); Qwen3.5-27B was the most conservative (77.97\%), a difference of 5.9 percentage points. REFUTED was rarely assigned by any model (0.13\%--1.68\%). The procedure therefore separates notes that document a coded condition from notes that do not provide evidence for it; it should not be used to conclude that a patient does not have the condition.

Support rates varied by disease (Table~\ref{tab:s_llm_disease}). The share of pairs with at least three SUPPORTED votes ranged from 58.2\% for chronic pain to 96.5\% for HIV. Agreement and support rates were not aligned. HIV had the highest support rate but the lowest \(\kappa\) (0.614), because when nearly all labels are SUPPORTED, expected chance agreement is high and \(\kappa\) is reduced. Obesity had a lower support rate (68.1\%) but the highest \(\kappa\) (0.895), indicating that the models agreed on which obesity codes lacked documentary support. A low \(\kappa\) should therefore be read alongside the support rate rather than as evidence of unreliable adjudication.

Support declined with the position of the qualifying code in the diagnosis sequence (Table~\ref{tab:s_llm_icd}). At least three models supported 94.1\% of principal-diagnosis pairs, 83.9\% of middle-position pairs, and 72.0\% of late-position pairs, and the share with no SUPPORTED votes rose from 2.4\% to 11.8\% and 22.2\%. Principal diagnoses accounted for only 5.7\% of adjudicated pairs, so most disease-cohort membership rested on secondary codes.

Verifying disease cohorts with the LLMs left the direction of every disease finding unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:s_llm_layer3}). Obesity and diabetes widened the adjusted BHC vs DI gap, and serious mental illness and sickle cell disease narrowed it, in the ICD-coded cohorts and under both vote thresholds. Two cohorts changed in their false discovery classification. Substance use disorder, which was not significant in the ICD-coded cohort (RR, 0.910; $q=0.161$), showed a significantly narrower gap after verification (RR, 0.829; $q=0.018$ with five votes; RR, 0.825; $q=0.010$ with three or more). HIV attenuated from 1.470 ($q=0.038$) to 1.396 with five votes (95\% CI, 0.996-1.96; $q=0.070$) and no longer met the false discovery threshold ($q=0.057$ with three or more votes). End-stage renal disease and chronic pain remained null. With cohorts verified by all five LLMs, the findings for obesity, diabetes, substance use disorder, serious mental illness, and sickle cell disease met the false discovery threshold under all 10 sentence-window encoders; HIV widened the gap under all 10 but met the threshold under 6, and end-stage renal disease and chronic pain met it under none.

\section{Supplementary Note: Data Processing and Variable Definitions}
\label{sec:s_processing}

This note documents each processing step between the source data and the analysis dataset, together with the code lists used to define disease cohorts and comorbidity. Counts refer to the primary sentence-window analysis.

\subsection{Source data}

Discharge summaries came from the discharge file of MIMIC-IV-Note version 2.2 (doi:10.13026/1n74-ne17), read with Latin-1 encoding. The patients, admissions, diagnoses\_icd, and d\_icd\_diagnoses tables came from the hosp module of MIMIC-IV version 3.1 (doi:10.13026/kpb9-mt58). Combining the two releases explains the 20 notes that could not be linked: their admissions are present in MIMIC-IV version 2.2 but were removed from version 3.1.
The stigmatizing-language resource of Harrigian and colleagues (version 1.0.0) supplied the 89-keyword lexicon and the 5,043 annotated training instances.

\subsection{Section segmentation and cohort construction}

Each discharge summary was divided into sections using regular expressions for 25 fixed section headers. A header was recognized only at the start of a line and when followed by a colon; matching ignored case, and longer headers were matched before shorter ones. Subheadings within the physical examination, such as HEENT, were deliberately not treated as section boundaries. When a header occurred more than once in a note, only the first occurrence was kept. Words were counted as maximal runs of word characters (letters, digits, or underscore), and section length was the number of such words.

A note entered the section-analysis cohort if it contained both a BHC and a DI section with at least 50 words each (Table~\ref{tab:s_flow}). The Follow-up Instructions section is replaced by an empty string during deidentification in MIMIC-IV-Note and was therefore excluded. The Social History section averaged about three words per note and was not used in the primary models.

\subsection{Keyword candidates and scoring}

The 89 lexicon keywords fall into the three non-overlapping families defined by Harrigian and colleagues: Credibility \& Obstinacy (13 terms), Compliance (26 terms), and Descriptors (50 terms). Keyword matching ignored case and required that the match not be preceded or followed by a letter, digit, or underscore. A space within a multiword term could match either a space or a hyphen, so that drug seeking also matched drug-seeking, and longer terms were matched before shorter ones. Because a hyphen counts as a boundary under this rule, non-compliance was matched as the term compliance rather than as noncompliance; whether such a match was stigmatizing was left to the classifier.

For scoring, each candidate was represented by the sentence containing the keyword and one sentence on either side, taken from the original section text. Sentence boundaries were a period, question mark, or exclamation mark followed by whitespace. The window was then cleaned and normalized with the procedures released with the annotation resource, and the keyword was located again in the normalized text. When the keyword occurred more than once in the window, the occurrence nearest the window midpoint was marked. Inputs longer than 256 model tokens were truncated, and each candidate received the label with the higher predicted score. Candidates whose keyword could not be found after normalization were excluded from event counts (Table~\ref{tab:s_candidates}). When the same keyword occurred twice within one three-sentence window, both candidates were marked at the same occurrence, so one of the two was anchored to the wrong position.

\subsection{Classifier training data}

The coordinates of the 5,043 annotations are defined on text processed with the cleaning and normalization procedures released with the resource. After applying the same procedures, every annotated keyword was located (5,043 of 5,043). Each training input contained the keyword sentence and one sentence on either side, with the keyword enclosed by the reserved tokens [unused1] and [unused2]. Negative, disbelief, and difficult annotations formed the stigma class; neutral, positive, and exclude annotations formed the non-stigma class. Instances were split 80/10/10 by note identifier with a fixed random seed of 42, giving 4,037 training, 501 validation, and 505 test instances.

Two features of the training data bear on interpretation. First, the split was made by note rather than by patient, so different notes from the same patient could fall in the training and test sets. Second, 3,552 of the 4,710 annotated notes were also in the 259,207-note analysis cohort, about 1.4\% of the cohort, so the classifier scored some passages it had seen during training.
Refitting the models without these notes did not change any conclusion (Table~\ref{tab:s_overlap}).

\subsection{Analysis dataset}

The analysis dataset contained one row for each BHC, HPI, and DI section of each cohort note: 259,207 BHC rows, 259,207 DI rows, and 252,581 HPI rows, for 770,995 rows in total. Notes without an HPI section had no HPI row. Stigmatizing-language events were counted for each note and section and merged onto these rows, and sections with no events were kept with a count of zero (713,965 rows).

Every note was linked to its patient and admission identifiers without loss. Twenty notes (57 rows) had no matching patient or admission record because their admissions had been removed from MIMIC-IV version 3.1, and therefore lacked sex and age; these were removed in the final complete-case step. The remaining covariates were coded as follows.

Medicaid status was 1 when the recorded insurance was Medicaid and 0 for Medicare, private, other, no charge, or missing insurance (missing for 2,793 notes). Limited English proficiency was 1 for any recorded language other than English, including the category Other (677 admissions), and missing language was coded as English (398 notes). Age was the anchor age plus the difference between the admission year and the anchor year, truncated to 18--91 years and centered at the sample mean. Length of stay was discharge time minus admission time, with negative values set to zero, and entered the models as \(\log(1+\text{days})\).

Race and ethnicity were assigned from the recorded category by checking, in order, for Black, Hispanic or Latino, Asian, White, and American Indian or Alaska Native or Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander; all remaining values were coded as Unknown. The Unknown group (19,075 admissions) comprised Other (8,709), Unknown (6,816), Patient Declined (1,082), Portuguese (989), Unable to Obtain (922), South American (302), and Multiple Race (255). The Other group (714 admissions) comprised American Indian or Alaska Native (503) and Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander (211).

For modeling, BHC was coded 1 and DI 0, and the offset was the log of the section word count with a floor of one word. The complete-case step on race and ethnicity, sex, Medicaid status, limited English proficiency, age, and word count removed 57 rows from 20 notes, all of them the unlinked notes above. The final group-analysis dataset contained 259,187 notes (one per admission) from 125,271 patients, with 518,374 BHC and DI rows, of which 481,383 had zero events, and 770,938 rows across all three sections.

\subsection{Disease cohort definitions}

Disease indicators were derived from all diagnosis codes recorded for each admission (Table~\ref{tab:s_disease_codes}). MIMIC-IV stores codes without decimal points; codes were stripped of surrounding whitespace and converted to upper case before matching. Code lists were applied separately by ICD version, so ICD-9 prefixes were matched only against ICD-9 codes and ICD-10 prefixes only against ICD-10 codes. A code was positive when it began with an inclusion prefix and did not begin with an exclusion prefix. An admission was assigned a condition when any of its codes was positive, regardless of the code's position in the diagnosis sequence; admissions had up to 57 codes. Ninety-one admissions had no recorded diagnosis codes and were coded as having none of the eight conditions and a Charlson score of zero.
Five-model verification removed most admissions that entered a cohort through codes outside its intended definition. Of 354 pairs that qualified for obesity only through overweight codes (278.02, E66.3), 30 were verified, and those notes documented obesity; of 13 pairs that qualified for serious mental illness only through depressive codes (296.82, 298.0), 1 was verified. Verification could not recover cases that the code lists never included. ICD-9 alcohol-induced mental disorders (291), which correspond to ICD-10 F10 codes, were not included in substance use disorder (202 admissions), and the exclusion of 282.64, which denotes sickle-cell/Hb-C disease with crisis rather than trait, removed 9 admissions from the sickle cell cohort.

\subsection{LLM verification protocol}
\label{sec:s_llm_protocol}

The unit of verification was an admission--disease pair: an admission in the analysis cohort with at least one diagnosis code in the cohort's code list (Table~\ref{tab:s_disease_codes}). Several codes for the same disease on one admission formed a single pair, giving 164,770 pairs. Each discharge summary was split at its section headers, and the models saw seven sections: Past Medical History, Chief Complaint, Discharge Diagnosis, Pertinent Results, Physical Exam, Medications on Admission, and Discharge Medications. These were concatenated in that order under their headers and truncated to 6,000 characters. Brief Hospital Course and Discharge Instructions were withheld because the stigma outcome is measured in them, so that verification of the exposure could not depend on the outcome text; History of Present Illness and Social History were withheld because they are analyzed elsewhere in the study. The ICD code, its descriptor, its position in the diagnosis list, and the number of codes on the admission were never shown to the models. Each model received only a fixed written definition of the disease (Table~\ref{tab:s_llm_definitions}).

The prompt consisted of a system message and a user message, reproduced verbatim below; \texttt{\{desc\}} is the disease definition and \texttt{\{evidence\}} the excerpts.

\begin{quote}
\small\raggedright
\textit{System message.} You are a careful clinical chart reviewer. You judge only what the provided excerpts actually document. You never infer a diagnosis from indirect hints such as a medication that has multiple indications, a body habitus remark, or a risk factor. You answer strictly in the requested JSON form and nothing else.

\smallskip
\textit{User message.} A billing ICD code on this hospital admission asserts the patient has: \texttt{\{desc\}}. Below are excerpts from the discharge summary. The narrative sections written during the hospital course were deliberately withheld; judge only from what is here. \texttt{--- BEGIN EXCERPTS --- \{evidence\} --- END EXCERPTS ---} Decide whether these excerpts document the condition above. ``SUPPORTED'': the excerpts affirmatively state or unambiguously document the condition (e.g. it is named in a problem list, past medical history, or discharge diagnosis). ``REFUTED'': the excerpts address this clinical area and are inconsistent with the condition. ``INSUFFICIENT'': the excerpts simply never speak to the condition either way. Only SUPPORTED counted toward verification; REFUTED and INSUFFICIENT were treated alike. REFUTED was rare (0.5\% of Qwen3.5-27B verdicts; Table~\ref{tab:s_llm_models}), so in practice the task was binary.

Five open-weight instruction-tuned models were run locally, and no text left the secure computing environment, as required by the PhysioNet data use agreement (Table~\ref{tab:s_llm_versions}). Inference used vLLM 0.26.0 for offline batched inference (PyTorch 2.11.0, CUDA 12.9) on one NVIDIA A100 80 GB GPU per job, with model weights in their native precision and no quantization. Decoding was greedy (temperature 0, random seed 0) and constrained to a JSON schema requiring the four fields above, with the verdict restricted to the three labels. The maximum number of new tokens was 200, or 1,024 for gpt-oss-20b, whose reasoning tokens share the output budget; its verdict was read from the final output channel. Reasoning mode was disabled for Qwen3.5-27B, and gpt-oss-20b used its default reasoning with the vLLM gpt-oss reasoning parser. The context window was 8,192 tokens (6,144 for Gemma-4-31B; some early Qwen3.5-27B and GLM-4-32B batches used 4,096). No prompt exceeded its model's context window, so no pair was skipped or truncated by the context limit.
